# Writing the guidance

Some of the framework's requirements cannot be measured from a workspace. They are questions, and the
Answers page asks them one at a time with the control title for context and nothing else:

> **Standardize DevOps processes (CI/CD)** — is this practice in place?

Nobody can answer that honestly. It does not say which standard is being held up, so the answer
depends on how generous the reader is feeling about their own estate — and once it becomes a score,
the generous answer is indistinguishable from the true one. An unanswered requirement reports as
unanswered, which is a fact somebody can act on. A guessed answer reports as evidence.

Guidance is what closes that gap. Each requirement gets: what the practice means, why it matters as
the risk of not doing it, what good looks like as the rubric an answer is measured against, three
worked examples so a reader can place themselves honestly, where to look to check, the ways it is
commonly got wrong, and when "partially" is the true answer.

**Every requirement, not only the ones a person is asked about.** That was the reach until L1b, on
the reasoning that a measured requirement needs no help being answered. It was the wrong set twice
over: a finding that says "pass" and stops leaves a reader with a score and no path, and the asked
set was never the required set anyway — measured on 2026-08-10, this app can put 125 of the 184 to a
person and 62 of those carried nothing.

This document is how to write one. The mechanics are ten minutes; the standard is the rest of it.

## Where it lives

`app/config/guidance/<pillar>.yaml`, one file per pillar, keyed by control id.

Not in `app/config/controls/` — those files are generated by `scripts/seed-catalogue.mjs` and say so
at the top. Authored prose in a generated file is prose a re-seed eventually eats.

The fields are structured rather than one markdown blob, which is a deliberate constraint on the
writing. It means every question reads the same way regardless of who wrote it, it lets the schema
require the parts that matter so nothing ships half-written, and it keeps a sanitisation decision out
of a content file — the panel renders text through React, which escapes it, and there is no markdown
parser anywhere near this path.

## The mechanics

```
npm run guidance:scaffold      # write a skeleton for every requirement that has none
npm run check:guidance         # schema, catalogue agreement, and the two coverage floors
```

The scaffold writes `status: draft` with the field names and a comment for each, so authoring is only
prose. Nothing in the app shows a draft: `authoredGuidance()` returns nothing for one, and the panel
says no guidance has been written for this requirement yet. That sentence is more useful than a
heading with an empty body under it — one tells a reader to go and ask a colleague, the other reads
as a bug.

When an entry is finished, change `status` to `authored` and raise the number in
`config/guidance/authored.json` in the same change. That number is a ratchet: `check:guidance`
refuses to let it fall, so a pillar that gets written stays written. It is raised by hand because a
script that raised it automatically would be a script that lowered it automatically. There are two of
them — `authored` for the nine original fields and `advised` for the six below — because every entry
written before 2026-08-10 was authored against a contract that did not have the six, and one number
would have reported the whole corpus as incomplete on the day the schema changed.

`status: authored` is the assertion, and the schema holds you to it — an authored entry must carry
all nine required fields at their minimum lengths, or the check fails. This is the one rule worth
internalising, because the failure it prevents is the one that would reach a customer: an entry with
three fields written renders as a panel of headings with nothing beneath them, looks like a defect in
the product rather than work not yet done, and is worse than the sentence admitting nothing was
written.

Three more rules apply to the prose itself, and all three were measured against the corpus before
being written rather than picked: every authored entry cites at least one reference, an entry that
names a regulation cites a reference that names it too, and no two entries may say nearly the same
thing in `means`, `matters`, `partial_when` or `start_from`.

The middle one is the one to know while writing the security pillar. "GDPR requires this" beside a
link to the cluster policy page satisfies the citation rule and is still this project telling a
customer what the law requires of them, so the citation has to be of the obligation. Nothing checks
that the source says what you say it says — describe the practice and let the regulation be the
reason somebody is asking, or cite the regulation and be right.

The last is a similarity bar of 50%; the closest pair anybody has actually written scores 32%, so
writing about two related requirements does not trip it and pasting one entry over another does.

## Advice: the six dimensions

`advice` is a separate block, and it answers a different question. The nine fields above help
somebody *answer*; these six tell them what to *do* — which is what a passing finding needs, and what
a failing one needs beyond the remediation step.

| Field | What it holds |
| --- | --- |
| `start_from` | The recommended position for a customer with no policy of their own |
| `depends_on` | The decision factors that would move them off it |
| `path` | Ordered steps from observed state to the target |
| `costs` | What the path costs — money, latency, a team's time, a constraint accepted |
| `retain` | The artefact worth keeping as evidence the practice is real |
| `revisit` | The event that should reopen the decision |

The block is optional and complete once present: all six or none. The reason is the same one behind
`status: authored`, at a smaller scale — a starting position with no trade-offs beside it is a
recommendation with its cost hidden, and a path with no review trigger is a project rather than a
practice.

`start_from` is a position, not a survey. "Choose the isolation model that fits your organisation" is
a survey. "One workspace per environment, catalogs per domain within it" is a position, and
`depends_on` is where the survey goes.

`costs` is where an honest recommendation earns its trust. If the path introduces no cost at all, the
starting position was probably too weak to be worth writing down.

## The standard

**Write the rubric, not the requirement.** `good` is the part that does the work. A reader compares
their estate against those signals and the comparison decides their answer, so each one has to be
something they can look at and know. "Production code is version-controlled" is a signal. "DevOps
maturity is high" is a mood.

**`matters` is a consequence, not a virtue.** Not "governance is important". Rather: where production
is edited in place, there is no record of what changed, no way to put it back, and no test between a
typo and a wrong number in a report somebody trusts. If the sentence would survive being pasted under
a different control, it is not about this one.

**`partial` is the example that matters.** Without it everybody is strong. The strong example is easy
to write and reassuring to read; the partial one is where most real estates actually sit, and naming
it is what makes the honest answer available. Write it as a specific half-done thing — notebooks in
Git but deployment from a laptop, and one team still editing a schedule in the UI — not as "some but
not all".

**`verify` names a place, and says what to expect there.** "Check your cluster policies" is not a
step. "Compute > Policies, and the policies two teams actually use: a policy on each cluster rather
than an unrestricted one" is. Prefer a SQL statement or a CLI command where one exists, because a
reader can run it and paste the result into their answer. Where the answer genuinely lives in
somebody's head, say so with `how: by-hand` and name whose head.

**`pitfalls` includes the ways it looks right.** Tests that run on a pull request but do not block the
merge. Data replicated to a second region without the jobs, permissions and secrets that read it.
These are the entries that earn their place, because a reader who is doing the thing wrongly and
confidently will not recognise themselves in a list of obvious failures.

**Omit `not_applicable_when` unless it is true.** Most requirements always apply, and the schema does
not ask for it. An empty "not applicable when: N/A" hands a reader a door out of a question they
should be answering.

**Plain English, and no marketing.** Write the sentence a colleague would say to another colleague at
a whiteboard. The reader is a platform engineer under time pressure who has a page of these in front
of them; anything that reads like a brochure gets skimmed, and skimmed guidance produces the guessed
answer this whole phase exists to prevent.

## What a check holds, and what a reviewer has to

This section is the approved standard for contextual implementation guidance. The rubric and the checks
have different jobs: the rubric defines quality, while the checks hold only the parts a machine can verify.

Answered by measuring the 63 authored entries rather than by reading the rules, and the reading is
[`app/scripts/recordings/guidance-standard.json`](../../app/scripts/recordings/guidance-standard.json), taken
by `node scripts/measure-guidance-standard.mjs`.

**The schema holds most of it.** `good` needs two signals of fifteen characters, `examples` needs all three,
`verify` needs a step and every step needs `how`, `where` and — since `100a` — `expect`. `partial_when` is
required on an authored entry, `advice` is all six dimensions or none, `path` needs two stages and
`depends_on` two factors. An entry that passes `npm run check:guidance` has all of that, so none of it is
worth a reviewer's attention.

**`check-guidance.mjs` holds the things a schema cannot see**: that the backlog is scaffolded rather than
absent, that coverage never falls, that an authored entry cites something, that a named regulation is cited
rather than alluded to, that two entries do not say the same thing, and that `last_reviewed` is real and
recent.

**Everything else in "The standard" above is a judgement, and there is no check coming.** Whether a signal is
a signal or a mood, whether `matters` is a consequence or a virtue, whether the partial example is one you
have actually seen, whether a pitfall covers the ways it looks right — a reviewer decides those, one pillar at
a time, which is what the next section is for.

**Two rules were proposed and refuted, so please do not propose them again.** `100a` costed both.

- *`good` may not reword the control title.* The word-overlap metric divides by the shorter word set, and 37
  of the 184 titles reduce to three significant words or fewer. "Use caching" is two, so any signal sharing
  one word with it scores 50%. The highest score in the corpus is 75%, on `PE-04-01`'s "Test data matches
  production in volume, or in a known and stated ratio" — a concrete signal, marked down for being on topic.
- *Every entry needs a runnable `verify` step.* 56 of 63 have one. The other 7 are requirements answered by
  looking at a console page, and `ui` plus `by-hand` is the honest set of steps for them.

**One was measured and not adopted.** Every authored entry carries two references, so a floor of two would
cost nothing — and nobody has decided that two sources are needed, so enforcing it would invent a standard
rather than hold one. It stays available and unadopted, which is the difference between a bar somebody set
and a bar the corpus happens to clear.

## Reviewing a pillar

Guidance is authored a pillar at a time and reviewed a pillar at a time, because the quality question
is comparative: twelve entries read together
expose the one that restates its title, the one whose `good` is aspirational, and the two that give
the same advice. One entry read alone exposes none of that.

A reviewer's job on a pillar is to answer three questions:

1. Could somebody outside the platform team answer these from the guidance alone?
2. Does `partial_when` describe an estate you have actually seen?
3. Is any `verify` step one you could not follow without already knowing the answer?

Set `last_reviewed` when that pass happens. The date is not decoration — this content has to survive
years of framework revisions, and an entry that silently describes a two-year-old product is worse
than an absent one, because the reader has no way to tell.

## Worked examples

Two entries are authored as the shape to follow, chosen because they are the two kinds of *question*
in the set:

- `OE-01-03` (standardize DevOps processes) in `operational-excellence.yaml` — a practice question,
  where verification is mostly a matter of looking in the right places.
- `REL-04-04` (high availability and disaster recovery) in `reliability.yaml` — a capability question,
  where the honest answer turns on whether anybody has ever exercised the thing, and the guidance has
  to say so.

**Both are `attestation` requirements, and none of the 113 still to write is.** That is worth knowing
before reading them as templates: they are the shape for guidance that helps somebody *answer*, and
every remaining entry is guidance that explains what the app *found*. Measured 2026-08-18, the
backlog is 52 `system-table`, 59 `rest-api` and 2 `derived` — 113 auto-assessed, 0 attestation.

So read a third, and for most of the remaining work read it first:

- `DG-01-06` (allow easy data discovery) in `data-and-ai-governance.yaml` — a `system-table`
  requirement, where the app has already computed a share and the entry's job is to say what that
  number does and does not mean. Note what it does with the coverage query's blind spot: the SQL step
  can see whether a comment exists and not whether it says anything, so the by-hand step carries the
  half that decides the answer, and the caveat says so.

**`rest-api` has no worked example, and it is the largest remaining group.** 58 of those 59 entries
are in `security-compliance-and-privacy`, whose first content row owes the example — the shape differs
because the finding is a configuration read back rather than a proportion of an estate, so there is no
partial share to interpret and `partial_when` has to mean something else. Until that row lands, `DG-01-06`
is the closest available model.

For `advice`, read `CO-01-03` (SQL warehouse for SQL workloads) in `cost-optimization.yaml` and
`REL-04-04` again in `reliability.yaml`. They are the two ends of it: one where the starting position
is cheap and the trade-off is a constraint, one where the starting position costs real money and
`depends_on` carries most of the argument.
